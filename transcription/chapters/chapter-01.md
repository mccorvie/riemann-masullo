**1.1.2. Spaces of Schwartz-Bruhat functions.**

• $\mathcal S(G)$ the space of Schwartz-Bruhat functions on a Hausdorff locally compact abelian group $G$. We will be usually assuming $G$ is second-countable. The examples of main interest are $G=F_v,F_v^\times,\mathbf A_F,\mathbf A_{F,S}=\prod_{v\in S}F_v,C_F=\mathrm{GL}_1(\mathbf A_F)/F^\times,J_{F,S}=\mathrm{GL}_1(\mathbf A_{F,S})/\mathcal O_{F,S}^\times$.

• Assume $G$ is a second-countable $\mathbf R_{>0}$-modulated group. For $I\subset\mathbf R$ a non-empty interval, $\mathcal S_I(G)$ is the space of *$I$-exponentially decaying* Schwartz-Bruhat functions, i.e. $\phi\in C^\infty(G)$ such that $\lvert\cdot\rvert_G^a\phi\in\mathcal S(G)$ for all $a\in I$. We topologize $\mathcal S_I(G)$ with the locally convex inductive limit topology of the nuclear Fréchet topologies on $\mathcal S_I(k,G)$ for $k\subset G$ varying over the compact subgroups of $G$ whose orthogonal $k^\perp\subset\widehat G$ is compactly generated. The topology on $\mathcal S_I(k,G)$ is the subspace topology induced by the linear injection

(1.1.2.1)

$$
T:\mathcal S_I(k,G)\to\prod_{a\in I}\mathcal S(k,G)
$$

sending $\phi$ to $(\lvert\cdot\rvert_G^a\phi)_{a\in I}$. This is also $\mathcal S_I(\mathbf R_{>0})\widehat{\otimes}_\pi\mathcal S(G^1/k)$.

• For $I=\mathbf R$, we denote $\mathcal S_I(G)$ by $\mathcal S_{\mathrm E}(G)$, and call this the space of *exponentially decaying Schwartz-Bruhat functions on $G$*.

• Under the above assumptions on $G$, $\mathcal S_I(G)$ is a strict LF-space. Nuclear (this is used to get good properties of topological tensor products and to get a handle on continuous duals of quotient linear maps, such that they are linear topological embeddings; nuclearity often ensures that quotient maps lift bounded sets, which is the crux of the matter behind the good behavior of the continuous duals of quotient maps), complete (used for vector valued integration), Montel (used for the Heine-Borel property: to get constants in some estimates that are uniform on bounded sets of functions).

**1.1.3. Hardy-Sobolev spaces.** Let $I\subset\mathbf R$ be a compact interval, i.e. $I=[a,b]$ for $a,b\in\mathbf R$, with $a<b$. For $G$ a second-countable Hausdorff locally compact abelian group and an $\mathbf R_{>0}$-modulated group, we denote by

$$
W_I^{s,2}(G)
$$

the completion of $\mathcal S_{\mathrm E}(G)$ (since $I$ is compact, it is also the completion of $\mathcal S_I(G)$) with respect to the linear topology generated by the norm:

$$
\|\phi\|_{s,I}:=\left(\int_G|\phi(g)|^2\max\{\lvert g\rvert_G^{2a},\lvert g\rvert_G^{2b}\}(1+\lvert\log\lvert g\rvert_G\rvert^2)^{s/2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(g)\right)^{1/2}
$$

with $\mu$ a fixed choice of Haar measure on $G$.

**1.2.1. Fundamental Divisor Exact Sequence.** Consider the following situation initially envisioned by Connes, but, contrary to what Connes does (who works, instead, with $L^2$-completions throughout) working with the Hausdorff locally convex topological $\mathbf C$-vector spaces:

$$
0\to\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{1/2}\mathrm H_{0,\mathrm{cont}}(F^\times,\mathcal S_0(\mathbf A_F))\to\mathcal S_{\mathrm E}(C_F)\to\mathscr H_F\to 0
$$

and each of their $C_F^1$-isotypes, i.e.

$$
0\to\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{1/2}\mathrm H_{0,\mathrm{cont}}(\chi|_{C_F^1},\mathcal S_0(\mathbf A_F))\to\mathcal S_{\mathrm E}(C_F)(\chi|_{C_F^1})\to\mathscr H_F(\chi|_{C_F^1})\to 0
$$

these latter being nuclear Fréchet spaces.

The Mellin transform turns the second exact sequence into (forgetting about the uniformly Schwartz condition defining Schwartz-Bergman spaces):

$$
0\to L(s+1/2,\chi)((s+1/2)(s-1/2))^\delta\mathcal O(\mathbf C)\cap A\mathcal S(\mathbf C)\to A\mathcal S(\mathbf C)\to\prod_{z:L(z,\chi)=0}\mathbf C^{N(z)}\to 0
$$

where $N(\rho)$ is the multiplicity of $\rho$ as a zero of $L(s,\chi)$. Here $\delta=0$ if $\chi|_{C_F^1}\neq1$, and $\delta=1$ otherwise. This connection to the $L$-function requires all the Structure Theorems, and use of known analytic properties of the $L$-function (convexity bound, existence of a sequence of horizontal segments across the critical strip on which the reciprocal of $L_f(s,\chi)$ is defined and grows at most polynomially in the absolute ordinate).

This is the reason why I called this the “divisor exact sequence”: it mirrors the global sections of

$$
0\to\mathscr O_X(-D)\to\mathscr O_X\to\mathscr O_D\to 0
$$

for the Stein space $X=\mathbf C$, and $D=\operatorname{div}(L(s+1/2,\chi)((s+1/2)(s-1/2))^\delta)$.

Considering these spaces systematically is already new. Only Meyer did so to some limited extent.

*The following is completely new and was, instead, never done before.*

(1) Take a Hardy-Sobolev completion of all spaces in the above linearly and topologically exact sequence of isotypes, to get:

$$
0\to W_I^{s,2}(\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{1/2}\mathrm H_{0,\mathrm{cont}}(\chi|_{C_F^1},\mathcal S_0(\mathbf A_F)))\to W_I^{s,2}(C_F)(\chi|_{C_F^1})\to W_I^{s,2}\mathscr H_F(\chi|_{C_F^1})\to 0
$$

where each space is now a Hilbert space.

Say:

(a) $I=[-a,a]$ for $a>0$ fixed and arbitrary.

(b) $\varphi\in\mathcal D(C_F)$ is supported strictly over $C_F^{>1}$, i.e. over $C_F^{>1+\varepsilon}$ for some small enough $\varepsilon>0$.

(2) To fix ideas we choose $s=0$. The rightmost space gives the spectral side of the trace formula, which reads

$$
\sum_{\rho,I}N(\rho)M(\varphi)\left(\chi\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{\rho-1/2}\right)
$$

where the sum is over those zeros $\rho$ of $L(s,\chi)$ such that $\rho-1/2$ is contained in the interior of $\mathbf C_I$. If $s>0$, then zeros $\rho$ such that $\rho-1/2\in\pm a+i\mathbf R$ also contribute to the above expression, but with a multiplicity function $N_s(\rho)$ defined as:

$$
N_s(\rho):=\max\left\{N(\rho),\left[\frac{s-1}{2}\right]\right\}.

Explicitly, we can use the image in $W_I^{s,2}\mathscr H_F(\chi|_{C_F^1})$ of the Schauder basis on $\mathscr H_F(\chi|_{C_F^1})$ to write down the operator

$$

$$

Here$\{\psi_{z,m}\}$ is the image in $W_I^{s,2}\mathscr H_F(\chi|_{C_F^1})$ of the dual Schauder basis in $\mathscr H_F(\chi|_{C_F^1})$ to $\{\delta^{(m)}_z\}_{(z,m)}$.

The convergence of the above displayed sum is in the sense of trace-class operators, i.e. in the trace-norm on the Hilbert space $W_I^{s,2}\mathscr H_F(\chi|_{C_F^1})$.

Since $\delta^{(m)}_z$ is the functional obtained by integration (with respect to $\bar\mu$ on $C_F$) of functions in $W_I^{s,2}(C_F)$ against the functions $\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{z-1/2}(\log\lvert\cdot\rvert_F)^m$, the finite rank kernels given by finite sums of $\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{z-1/2}(\log\lvert\cdot\rvert_F)^m\otimes\psi_{z,m}$ are actual smooth functions of two variables on $C_F\times C_F$, and their sum converges in $L^2(C_F\times C_F,\nu\otimes\nu^\vee)$, where

(1.2.1.1)

$$
\nu=\max\{\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{-2a},\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{2a}\}(1+\lvert\log\lvert\cdot\rvert_F\rvert^2)^{s/2}\bar\mu,\quad\nu^\vee=\frac{1}{\max\{\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{-2a},\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{2a}\}(1+\lvert\log\lvert\cdot\rvert_F\rvert^2)^{s/2}}\bar\mu.

$$

**1.2.3. Reduction to an $S$-local trace calculation.** In light of the previous point, the main Trace Formula now reads as follows:

$$
\int_{C_F}k_{S,\varphi}(x,x)\,\mathrm d\bar\mu(x)=-\int_{C_F}k_{\mathrm{spec},I,\varphi}(x,x)\,\mathrm d\bar\mu(x)=-\sum_{\rho,I}N(\rho)M(\varphi)\left(\chi|\cdot|_F^{\rho-1/2}\right).
$$

In turn, $\int_{C_F}k_{S,\varphi}(x,x)\,\mathrm d\bar\mu(x)$ is then computed using a version of the Connes-Meyer “$S$-local trace formula”, discussed in their papers.

Reducing to the $S$-local calculation of $\int_{C_F}k_{S,\varphi}(x,x)\,\mathrm d\bar\mu(x)$ is what this work is all about. Once the reduction is made, the problem is solved.

**1.8.1. The missed trip to Princeton from a decade ago.** Towards the end of my first year and the beginning of my second year as a Graduate Student at Stanford, inspired by $p$-adic Hodge Theory, the possibility occurred to me that one could use “Koszul complexes” of entire functions (a modern way to paraphrase what I was thinking) to make a trace calculation in a vein that would be hybrid between Connes’ dream and Deninger’s.

It all stemmed from the observation that if $F$ is a number field (with idele class group $C_F$), $\chi:C_F\to\mathbf C^\times$ is a quasi-character, and $S$ is a finite set of places of $F$ containing all Archimedean places, all places ramified over $\mathbf Q$, and all places at which $\chi$ is ramified then for a generic Schwartz-Bruhat function of the form

$$
f=f_S\otimes\bigotimes_{v\notin S}1_{\mathcal O_{F_v}}
$$

with $f_S\in\mathcal S(\prod_{v\in S}F_v)$, if we replace $1_{\mathcal O_{F_v}}$ with $1_{\mathcal O_{F_v}^\times}$ one finite place outside $S$ at a time we would slowly “degenerate” $f$ into $f_S$ in the sense that on the connected component $\Omega(\chi)$ of $\chi$ in the space of idelic quasi-characters the local contributions of $1_{\mathcal O_{F_v}^\times}$’s (for $v\notin S$) to the Mellin transform on $\Omega(\chi)$ are equal to 1 (when using the standard Haar measures).

At the $S$-local level, Connes already had a perfectly good technique to compute the relevant trace. His calculation is a reformulation of a calculation of Weil's from his papers on the Explicit Formulas, resting on the observation that a certain additive local Fourier transform of a logarithm function would be expressed as a principal value arising out of the Residue Theorem applied to the logarithmic derivative of the Hecke $L$-function.

In my eyes, a version of Connes' $S$-local calculation is definitive, i.e. at the “$S$-local level”, it is the right scenario to hope for indeed. I have come to view parts of the calculations in Connes' $S$-local “trace formula” as his most important contribution (I have a feeling he would get annoyed, if told).

And so the strategy “degenerate something global into something $S$-local” seems a good rule of thumb to follow. This is exactly what Ralf Meyer does in his paper [Me1]: he uses operators which, under Mellin transform, turn into multiplication operators by finite reciprocal Euler factors. For his method to succeed, he needs them to converge to infinite reciprocal Euler products (with a few finitely many Euler factors removed).

This is possible only by working with very fine locally convex strict-LF topologies (or ensuing bornologies, as he does) and indeed his method of degeneration from global to $S$-local pans out, and leads to a trace formula in the vein that Connes was partly hoping for.

Such topologies, however, render *all* point-masses (and their derivatives) at all zeros of the Hecke $L$-function, continuous, and so the resulting trace formula is just a copy of Weil's own Explicit Formulas. This continuity aspect concerning the point-masses and their derivatives at all zeros, is what makes all the zeros (whatever their location and multiplicity may be) contribute to the spectral side of the trace formula on equal footing.

Now look at the situation with our chosen Schwartz-Bruhat function. We choose an enumeration $\{v_k\}_{k\ge 0}$ of all the places of $F$ outside $S$.

Assume we have fixed a continuous (unitary) character $\chi\in\widehat{C_F}$, and that $S$ contains all the Archimedean places, all the primes at which $\chi$ is ramified, and all the ramified places of $F$ over $\mathbf Q$ (for other technical reasons it is also important in the main work to make $S$ big enough to ensure $\mathrm{Pic}(\mathcal O_{F,S})=1$, but the need for this isn't addressed here). Going from $f=f_S\otimes(\otimes_{v\notin S}1_{\mathcal O_{F_v}})$ restricted to the idele group to

$$
f':=f_S\otimes\otimes\bigotimes_{k\ge 0}1_{\mathcal O_{F_{v_k}}^\times} \tag{1.8.1.1}
$$

as a function on the idele group can either be done one step at a time, or infinitely many steps at a time. This latter is what Meyer does, roughly speaking.

Each time we replace $1_{\mathcal O_{F_{v_k}}}$ with $1_{\mathcal O_{F_{v_k}}^\times}$ for some $k$, the effect on the zeta integral of the Schwartz-Bruhat function on the connected component $\{\chi|\cdot|_F^s\}$ of $\chi$ is to remove the Euler factor at $v_k$, which is to say to multiply the zeta integral before such replacement by

$$
1-\chi(\mathfrak p_{v_k})q_{v_k}^{-s}, \tag{1.8.1.2}
$$

and so an infinite iteration of this process will converge as an operator (on the quotient of all $\chi|_{C_F^1}$-homogeneous adelic $\theta$-functions) only when we endow the function space with a topology that is matched under Mellin transform by a topology at least as fine as the topology of uniform convergence on all vertical strips of bounded width of $\mathbf C_{>1}=\{\sigma>1\}$ for $Z(f,\chi|\cdot|_F^s)$, and of $\mathbf C_{<0}=\{\sigma<0\}$ for $Z(\widehat f,\overline{\chi}|\cdot|_F^{1-s})$.

This is, loosely, what Meyer does: he degenerates a global convolution operator using infinite Euler products, makes a trace calculation following Connes at the $S$-local level, and observes that by enlarging $S$ by a finite amount the “part of the kernel” that he has left behind in the degeneration process contributes zero to the global trace. Convergence of infinite Euler products is crucial for him to prove this last fact.

The obstruction to repeating what Meyer does, but by working in normed completions such as the $L^2$-norm topology on $\mathcal S(C_F)$, is that infinite Euler products do not converge absolutely within the critical strip, by Mertens' Theorem.

The advantage of proving a global trace formula while working with respect to much coarser normed topologies as my work does, as opposed to the very fine limit-Fréchet topologies or bornologies that Meyer has to work with to make infinite Euler product operators converge, is enormous because the spectral side of such a global trace formula is affected by the choice of topology: not all zeros support point-masses (and their derivatives) that extend by continuity!

Mertens' Theorem is, in my eyes, a herald for GRH. If you clash against it, it means that you are entering forbidden territory: it means you have to come up with something new.

What occurred to me is the following: what if instead of doing this wished-for degeneration process from global to $S$-local by using an infinite iteration of $1_{\mathcal O_{F_{v_k}}}\mapsto 1_{\mathcal O_{F_{v_k}}^\times}$ to reach (1.8.1.1) (equivalently, to infinitely iterate multiplication by terms as in (1.8.1.2)), which is what Meyer does (I am paraphrasing a lot for him), *we did it one step at a time only*?

1.8.3.1.  *Fundamental Divisor Exact Sequence.* Consider the following situation, initially envisioned by Connes, but, contrary to what Connes does (who works, instead, with $L^2$-completions throughout) working with the Hausdorff locally convex topological $\mathbf C$-vector spaces:

$$
0\to\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{1/2}\mathrm H_{0,\mathrm{cont}}(F^\times,\mathcal S_0(\mathbf A_F))\to\mathcal S_{\mathrm E}(C_F)\to\mathscr H_F\to 0

and each of their $C^1_F$-isotypes, i.e.

$$
0\to\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{1/2}\mathrm H_{0,\mathrm{cont}}(\chi|_{C^1_F},\mathcal S_0(\mathbf A_F))\to\mathcal S_{\mathrm E}(C_F)(\chi|_{C^1_F})\to\mathscr H_F(\chi|_{C^1_F})\to 0 \tag{1.8.3.1}
$$

these latter being nuclear Fréchet spaces.

The presence of the quasi-character $\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{1/2}$ in front of the space of global adelic $\theta$-functions means the following: we consider $\mathrm H_{0,\mathrm{cont}}(F^\times,\mathcal S_0(\mathbf A_F))$ as a subspace of $\mathcal S_{\mathrm E}(C_F)$. It is a *closed* subspace, and a continuous sub-representation of $C_F$. This is a consequence of the five Structure Theorems alluded to earlier. The space $\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{1/2}\mathrm H_{0,\mathrm{cont}}(F^\times,\mathcal S_0(\mathbf A_F))$ is the image of $\mathrm H_{0,\mathrm{cont}}(F^\times,\mathcal S_0(\mathbf A_F))$ under the multiplication operator by the quasi-character $\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^{1/2}$ (this multiplication operator is a linear topological automorphism on $\mathcal S_{\mathrm E}(C_F)$. The Mellin transform turns it into a shift by $1/2$ to the left, on the level of entire functions, Schwartz uniformly on vertical strips of bounded width).

We parametrize the Riemann surface $\Omega$ of all idelic quasi-characters as $\{\chi\lvert\cdot\rvert_F^s\}$ as $\chi$ varies over a choice of unitary base point for the connected components of $\Omega$ (which we choose to be the of finite order the connected component contains a quasi-character with finite image).

The Mellin transform turns the second exact sequence into:

$$
0\to L(s+1/2,\chi)((s+1/2)(s-1/2))^\delta\mathcal O(\mathbf C)\cap A\mathcal S(\mathbf C)\to A\mathcal S(\mathbf C)\to V_{\chi|_{C^1_F}}\to 0 \tag{1.8.3.2}
$$

where $N(\rho)$ is the multiplicity of $\rho$ as a zero of $L(s,\chi)$. Here $\delta=0$ if $\chi|_{C^1_F}\ne1$, and $\delta=1$ otherwise.

The quotient $V_{\chi|_{C^1_F}}$ carries a Schauder basis satisfying several good properties: its dual basis is given by the point-masses at the zeros of $L(s+1/2,\chi)$ and their derivatives.

This connection to the $L$-function requires all the Structure Theorems, and use of known analytic properties of the $L$-function (convexity bound, existence of a sequence of horizontal segments across the critical strip on which the reciprocal of $L_f(s,\chi)$ is defined and grows at most polynomially in the absolute ordinate. In the process of describing the image of $\mathrm H_{0,\mathrm{cont}}(F^\times,\mathcal S_0(\mathbf A_F))$ under the Mellin transform, we must also draw from Bohr’s theory of almost periodic functions, taken to a fuller expression in Bagchi’s Ph.D. thesis, to prove that finite Euler products of non-Archimedean local factors admit a sequence of horizontal segments whose ordinates tend to $\pm\infty$ respectively, and on which they are bounded, in order to perform asymptotic contour integration. This can also be done using the Kronecker-Weyl Theorem).

The sequence (1.8.3.2) is the reason why I titled this subsection the “divisor exact sequence”: (1.8.3.2) mirrors the global sections of

$$
0\to\mathscr O_X(-D)\to\mathscr O_X\to\mathscr O_D\to 0
$$

for the Stein space $X=\mathbf C$, and $D=\mathrm{div}(L(s+1/2,\chi)((s+1/2)(s-1/2))^\delta)$.
